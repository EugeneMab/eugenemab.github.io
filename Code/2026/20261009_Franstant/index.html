Franstant (Français Constant)

An artificial language built upon French without conjugation — by Jian Huang

1. Introduction

Franstant (Français Constant) is an artificial language built upon French. "Constant" means that every word keeps an invariable form — verbs do not conjugate, nouns do not take plural forms, adjectives do not agree in gender or number. The goal is to radically simplify grammar while retaining the vocabulary, phonetics, and cultural spirit of French.

The core design principles are:

1. Constant verbs: Verbs always remain in the infinitive. Tense is expressed through modal words (fit / a fait = past, fera = future; see Ch. 5).
2. Constant nouns: Nouns stay in the base masculine singular form. Plurality is indicated only by the article.
3. No contractions: All elisions and contractions are removed. Every word is written in full.
4. Fixed word order: Strict Subject-Verb-Object order. Adjectives are always placed after the noun.
5. Unsplit negation: "ne pas" appears together before the verb.
6. Strict pronunciation: Pronunciation follows only the general rules, with no exceptions: every word is pronounced exactly as its letters predict (see Ch. 6).

2. Detailed Rules and Examples

2.1 Articles

Definite articles reduce to le (singular) and les (plural). "la" and "l'" are removed. Indefinite articles reduce to un (singular) and des (plural). "une" is removed. The partitive article is uniformly du (meaning "some"). Note that de le is reserved for "of the" (possession) and never contracts to "du".

All nouns strictly use the base masculine singular form. There are no irregular plurals. Plurality is indicated only by the articles "les" or "des" — the noun itself never changes.

Subject pronouns use the disjunctive (tonic) form. Humans and animals distinguish gender: lui (masculine) and elle (feminine). Non-human, non-animal entities (objects, places, concepts, and impersonal constructions) use on uniformly without gender (equivalent to "it" / "one"). 2.5 Verbs and Tense

Verbs always remain in the infinitive without any conjugation. Tense is expressed through modal words of "faire" (see Chapter 5 for the full table).

2.6 Negation

ne jamais means “never” and also stays together before the verb or modal word. Example: moi ne jamais dire on.

Ordinary negation uses ne pas as one block before the verb (or modal word). ne rien (nothing) and ne personne (nobody) also negate: ne stays directly before the negated word, and the phrase occupies that word's normal sentence position, without adding ne pas before the verb.

Examples
Je ne mange pas. → moi ne pas manger.
Il n'est pas venu. → lui ne pas a fait venir.
Tu ne partiras pas. → toi ne pas fera partir.
Je ne sais rien. → moi savoir ne rien.
Tu ne vois personne. → toi voir ne personne.

2.7 Adjectives, Comparatives and Superlatives

Adjectives are fixed to the masculine singular form and always placed after the noun. Articles and possessives remain at the beginning of the noun phrase.

Comparatives and Superlatives: Always strictly regular without exception; irregular forms are never used. Comparatives consistently use plus (more) and moins (less); superlatives consistently use le plus (the most) and le moins (the least) (or les plus / les moins in plural). Irregular forms such as "meilleur", "pire", and "moindre" are completely abolished and replaced by regular plus bon, plus mauvais, le plus bon, le plus mauvais, etc. The comparative/superlative modifier always follows the noun.

2.8 Prepositions

Prepositions never contract with articles. All contracted forms are written separately.

2.9 Questions

Questions uniformly use être ce que. "Est-ce que" is not used (because "est" is conjugated), nor are hyphens or elisions.

Examples
Est-ce que tu vas au restaurant ? → être ce que toi aller à le restaurant ?
Est-ce qu'il est ici ? → être ce que lui être ici ?

2.10 Passive Voice

The passive voice is expressed by the modal word est fait (see Chapter 5). Past passive uses fit est fait or a fait est fait.

Examples
Le livre est lu. → le livre est fait lire.
La maison a été construite par son père. → le maison a fait est fait construire par son père.

2.11 Reflexive Verbs and Pronominal Voice

All reflexive pronouns become se, which must be placed immediately before the infinitive (se + infinitive, inseparable). The pronominal voice has the exact same form as the reflexive. Tense modals precede se, as in moi fit se laver. In everyday present tense, the modal is omitted: moi se laver.

Examples
Je me lave. → moi se laver.
Je me lavai. → moi fit se laver.
Ils se trouvent là-bas. → eux se trouver là.

2.12 Existence

Il y a is uniformly replaced by voilà.

Il y a beaucoup d'enfants. → voilà beaucoup de enfant.

4. Numbers

Franstant thoroughly regularizes the French numerical system into a clean base-10 decimal system. The irregular Latin-derived forms for 11 to 16 are replaced by regular compounds dix un to dix six. The vigesimal (base-20) relics for 70, 80, and 90 (60+10, 4×20, 4×20+10) are replaced by pure decimal terms septante, huitante, and nonante. All compound numbers are separated by simple spaces without hyphens or "et". Numbers remain invariable without plural -s.

5. Verb Tense and Voice (Modal System of "faire")

To precisely express verb tenses and voices, Franstant adopts the conjugated forms of the verb faire as a pre-verbal modal auxiliary, while the main verb always remains in its infinitive form.

Core Design Principles:
1. Preference for 3rd Person Singular: By default, 3rd-person singular (il/elle) forms are selected for consistency.
2. Phonetic Disambiguation (4 Exceptions *): Whenever a 3rd-person form produces homophony within the faire paradigm, another person is selected, ensuring 24 strictly unique pronunciations.
3. Default Unmarked Present Tense: In standard usage, the present tense requires no modal auxiliary; the bare infinitive denotes the present tense. The modal "font" is reserved for complete paradigm alignment or explicit emphasis.

5.2 Syntax and Examples

Word order is fixed: [Subject] + (ne pas) + [modal word] + [(se) + main infinitive] + [object/adjunct]. se and the infinitive cannot be split. Ordinary present tense omits the modal. ne rien and ne personne occupy the negated constituent's position.

Present (Default, Unmarked)
moi manger du pain. — I eat bread (standard present requires no modal auxiliary).
Passé Composé (a fait)
moi a fait manger du pain. — I ate bread (Standard French: J'ai mangé du pain).
Imparfait (faisait)
moi faisait lire un livre quand lui a fait entrer. — I was reading a book when he came in (Standard French: Je lisais un livre quand il est entré).
Futur Simple (fera)
nous fera voyager à Paris demain. — We will travel to Paris tomorrow (Standard French: Nous voyagerons à Paris demain).
Conditional Present (ferait)
moi ferait aimer visiter ce musée. — I would like to visit this museum (Standard French: J'aimerais visiter ce musée).
Subjunctive Present (fasse)
on falloir que toi fasse comprendre. — You must understand (Standard French: Il faut que tu comprennes).
Passive Voice (est fait)
le lettre est fait écrire par Pierre. — The letter is written by Pierre (Standard French: La lettre est écrite par Pierre).
Pronominal Voice (se + Infinitive, Identical to Reflexive)
lui se lever tôt chaque matin. — He gets up early every morning (Standard French: Il se lève tôt chaque matin; present needs no modal, se immediately before verb).
moi fit se laver. — I washed myself (Standard French: Je me lavai; tense modal fit placed before se).

Final Consonants and Terminal Rules

A word-final consonant is pronounced only if it is c, r, f, or l; every other final consonant is silent. The following terminal rules take precedence over that rule: final -er and -ez sound [e] (manger, nez), except a monosyllabic word ending in -er, which sounds [ɛ] with r pronounced (mer [mɛʁ]); final -ent sounds [ɑ̃] (eussent, vent); final -x and final -q are silent (dix [di], cinq [sɛ̃]). For the full er and es rules, see Deterministic Rules below. No other ending is silenced by rule.

Deterministic Rules: x, er, es, ier, s, final -e, y

x: if word-final, silent (dix [di], paix [pɛ]); otherwise always [ks] (taxi [taksi], examen [ɛksamɑ̃]). The [ɡz] pronunciation is discarded.
er: (1) if the word has a single syllable, [ɛ] with r pronounced (mer [mɛʁ], fer [fɛʁ]); (2) else if word-final, [e] with r silent (manger [mɑ̃ʒe], parler [paʁle]); (3) else, e and r sound separately, [ɛ] + [ʁ] (merci [mɛʁsi], fermer [fɛʁme]).
es: (1) if the word has a single syllable, [ɛ] (les [lɛ], des [dɛ]); (2) else if word-final, [ə] (parles [paʁlə], tables [tablə]); (3) else, e and s sound separately: [ɛ] + [s] in a closed syllable (espoir [ɛspwaʁ]), [ə] + [z] in an open syllable (peser [pəze]).
ier: if word-final and the word is polysyllabic, [je] (premier [pʁəmje], papier [papje]); if word-final and the word is monosyllabic, [jɛʁ] (fier [fjɛʁ], hier [jɛʁ]).
s: [z] between two vowels, otherwise [s] (rose [ʁozə], sur [syʁ]); word-final s is silent by the CaReFuL rule.
Word-final -e sounds [ə] (table [tablə], parle [paʁlə]); the schwa may be dropped in fast speech, but the rule writes [ə].
y: between two vowels, y splits into ii, then the other rules apply (ayez → aiiez → [ɛje]; crayon → craiion → [kʁɛjɔ̃]).

Examples: Standard Exceptions Abolished

Standard French et [e] becomes [ɛ] (e in a closed syllable, final t silent); ville [vil] becomes [vijə] (ill = [ij]); mille [mil] becomes [mijə]; cinq [sɛ̃k] becomes [sɛ̃] (nasal in, final q silent); femme [fam] becomes [fɛmə] (e in a closed syllable); oignon [ɔɲɔ̃] becomes [waɲɔ̃] (oi = [wa]); second [səɡɔ̃] becomes [səkɔ̃] (c = [k] before o); dix [dis] becomes [di] and six [sis] becomes [si] (final x silent); eussent [ys] becomes [œsɑ̃] (eu = [œ] in a closed syllable, ss = [s], final -ent = [ɑ̃]).

Stress

As in French, the stress always falls on the last syllable of the rhythmic group.

Differences from Standard French

In standard French, three things break the letter-to-sound rules: elision drops the final vowel of certain words before a vowel (as in l'homme, d'eau); liaison pronounces a normally silent final consonant before a vowel (as in les amis, pronounced [le.za.mi]); and individual words carry exceptional pronunciations (et [e], ville [vil], cinq [sɛ̃k]). In Franstant, none of these three exists. Each word is pronounced independently and exactly by rule: "le homme" is pronounced [lə.ɔmə], "de eau" is pronounced [də.o], et is [ɛ], ville is [vijə], cinq is [sɛ̃]. While this creates hiatus, it is a deliberate design choice of Franstant to ensure clear word boundaries and fully predictable pronunciation.
